# Inverters

## What an inverter does

The inverter converts the direct current (DC) that solar panels produce into the alternating current (AC) that runs household appliances and feeds the grid. It's also usually where system monitoring, safety disconnects, and (for hybrid systems) battery charge/discharge management live.

## Types of inverters

- **String inverters:** one central inverter serves a "string" of panels wired in series. Cost-effective and the common default for straightforward roofs with uniform sun exposure. A shading or fault issue on one panel in the string can drag down the whole string's output.
- **Microinverters:** a small inverter per panel (or per pair of panels). More expensive per watt, but each panel operates independently — shading or an issue on one panel doesn't drag down the others. Better suited to roofs with partial shading or multiple orientations.
- **Hybrid inverters:** manage both grid-tied operation and battery charge/discharge in one unit, used for hybrid systems (see `grid-tied-vs-off-grid-vs-hybrid.md`). Off-grid systems use a related but distinct category of inverter/charge-controller setup, since there's no grid signal to synchronize with.

## Sizing and phase matching

Inverter capacity needs to be matched to the array's output and to the property's electrical service. Three-phase electrical supply — typical for larger commercial and industrial loads — needs a three-phase inverter; most residential single-phase homes use a single-phase inverter. Getting this wrong is a common source of underperforming systems, so electrical phase is one of the details EnergyWize always confirms.
